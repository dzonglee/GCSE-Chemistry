## Questions considered before building

What does this lesson need? Students must distinguish a metal from its compound, compare physical properties in the requested direction, and explain the limits of colour/catalyst evidence. A named physical-property table alone does not demonstrate chemical examples. Actual Maths explaining-scatter-conclusions source and live desktop/mobile guided views were inspected: its prediction and evidence-limit writing informed this lesson's approach.

What makes the interaction useful? A cobalt chloride paper model starts with a blank prediction, lets students change dry/wet conditions, and preserves wrong answers through reload, undo and reset. Its labelled colour card displays the student's prediction rather than pretending to show an experiment. The manganese(IV) oxide guide uses the existing rate graph/table with explicitly hypothetical results: faster early production does not imply a greater final amount. Neither simulation certifies practical competence.

What assets fit the demand? A readable condition/colour card and a rate graph communicate the relevant evidence. Spatial geometry is not needed to explain this paper observation. The course's existing real 3D assets remain available for lessons with spatial demands.

What mistakes or limits needed attention? Chromium solution colours are supplied, rather than an extra memorisation requirement. Different colours alone do not establish different ion charges or uniquely identify a substance. Blue dry/pink wet is specific to the supplied cobalt indicator-paper example, not every cobalt compound. Nickel metal and MnO₂ compound are catalysts in appropriate reactions, not universal catalysts. Physical comparison answers must actually compare melting/boiling point, density, strength or hardness; true chemical statements can miss the question.

Would this help a student? It gives concrete evidence, preserves misconceptions for review, and requires independent writing after guided work. This is a design judgment supported by selected materials and checks, not pupil-outcome evidence or whole-course exam-readiness certification.
